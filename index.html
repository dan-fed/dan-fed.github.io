Summary Indices in Treatment Effect Estimation: When Are They Useful?

PDF

The paper examines the common practice of combining multiple outcomes into a summary index to estimate a causal effect. For a common class of causal estimators, such estimates represent a weighted average of component-level effects, yet the weights are implicit in the construction procedures and are never reported. These weights are derived and analyzed: they can be negative and unrestricted in magnitude. In a simple model, negative weights arise naturally for components of the summary index more strongly associated with the underlying concept. The asymptotic distribution of the estimates is established. Conventionally reported confidence intervals can be invalid because they do not account for data-dependent construction of the summary index, yet the corrections are likely to be small in real applications. Since none of the commonly used summary index procedures incorporate the sources of variability relevant to the estimator's variance, the resulting estimates do not generally exhibit the high power often attributed to them. Yet, in a simple RCT-like scenario with homoskedasticity and a small treatment effect, it is possible to unambiguously rank the summary indices studied here by the mean squared error of the difference-in-means estimates. Data from three recent empirical studies illustrate the theoretical results. Some simple summary indices can be useful for simplifying the reporting of results and should be interpreted as an "average effect" on the components. Beyond this, the results provide no compelling reason to use summary indices. For serious empirical conclusions, analyzing and reporting effects on individual components is recommended.

Partially Identified Rankings from Pairwise Interactions

with Federico Crippa

arXiv

This paper considers the problem of ranking objects based on their latent merits using data from pairwise interactions. We allow for incomplete observation of these interactions and study what can be inferred about rankings in such settings. First, we show that identification of the ranking depends on a trade-off between the tournament graph and the interaction function: in parametric models, such as the Bradley-Terry-Luce, rankings are point identified even with sparse graphs, whereas nonparametric models require dense graphs. Second, moving beyond point identification, we characterize the identified set in the nonparametric model under any tournament structure and represent it through moment inequalities. Finally, we propose a likelihood-based statistic to test whether a ranking belongs to the identified set. We study two testing procedures: one is finite-sample valid but computationally intensive; the other is easy to implement and valid asymptotically. We illustrate our results using Brazilian employer-employee data to study how workers rank firms when moving across jobs.
